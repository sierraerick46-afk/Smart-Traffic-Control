Problem statement

In Chía the traffic lights usually repeat the same cycle all day, even when one street is empty and the other is full. But traffic is not always the same: it changes with the hour, the day of the week and the type of moment (off-peak, moderate or peak). On Avenida Pradilla, for example, about 40,000 vehicles pass every day and a trip that normally takes 7–10 minutes can take up to 40 in rush hour (El Cronista, 2026). When the green time does not match the traffic, cars wait more than necessary, green time is wasted on empty streets, and drivers lose time and fuel.

Guiding question: how can the traffic lights of four intersections in Chía adapt their green time to the traffic each one receives, without testing unproven ideas on real streets?

Justification

  • We have data to work with. Hourly vehicle counts for four junctions and a traffic-signal inventory can show patterns that a fixed plan ignores.
  • Each intersection is analyzed on its own. Every intersection has its own rush hours and its own queues, so one average for the whole town would hide them. The dashboard and the simulation show the results of each one separately.
  • Each part of the problem has a matching AI technique. Unsupervised learning finds the type of moment, supervised learning forecasts the traffic, and reinforcement learning decides the green time.
  • It is safe. The agent learns in a simulator and is compared with a fixed plan before anything is proposed for the street.
  • It is easy to extend. The app is split in layers (Controller, Service, Model), so each part can be tested and improved separately.

Scope: this is a simulation prototype and it does not control real traffic lights. The vehicle counts come from a public dataset that we assigned to four intersections of Chía as a case study, so the results show how the method works, not the real traffic of Chía.

General objective

Build a web prototype that uses artificial intelligence to adjust the green time of four intersections in Chía according to the traffic each one receives, and check in a simulation whether it works better than a fixed 30-second plan.

Specific objectives

  1. Clean and organize the hourly vehicle counts and the data of the four intersections, so each one can be analyzed on its own.
  2. Find the types of moment (off-peak, moderate and peak) of each intersection with the K-Means algorithm.
  3. Forecast the vehicles per hour with Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and an MLP, and compare the three models with MSE, MAE and R².
  4. Train a DQN agent in a simulated intersection (four approaches and two phases) that decides when to keep or change the green, and test it on each of the four intersections.
  5. Compare the agent with the 30-second fixed plan (average queue, vehicles served and vehicles lost) and publish the application on Render with a dashboard and a live simulation.

Theoretical

Unsupervised learning. K-Means puts similar hours in the same group without anyone telling it the groups first (MacQueen, 1967).

Supervised learning. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting build many small decision trees and combine them (Breiman, 2001; Friedman, 2001). The MLP is a neural network that learns by correcting its own errors (Rumelhart et al., 1986).

Reinforcement learning. An agent learns by trial and error to get the best reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018). A DQN uses a neural network to decide which action is best (Mnih et al., 2015).

Conceptual

Phase: set of approaches that receive green at the same time. Cycle: sequence of all phases. Green time: seconds a phase keeps the right of way.

Queue: vehicles waiting at an approach. Waiting time: time those vehicles spend stopped. Flow: vehicles that pass in one hour.

Fixed-time control repeats a pre-set plan (Webster, 1958); adaptive control adjusts it to the observed traffic (Wei et al., 2019).

CRISP-ML(Q) is a step-by-step method for machine learning projects, from understanding the problem to monitoring the model (Studer et al., 2021). Each of its six phases has its own page in the CRISP-ML menu.

Technological

Python backend with Flask and gunicorn; pandas and NumPy for the data; scikit-learn for K-Means, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting (Pedregosa et al., 2011); PyTorch for the MLP and the DQN (Paszke et al., 2019); HTML, CSS, JavaScript and Bootstrap 5 for the interface; a login to protect the dashboard and the simulation; Git and GitHub for teamwork (one branch per phase) and Render for deployment.

Contextual

Chía is a municipality of the Sabana Centro region, about 10 km north of Bogotá, and its roads fill up at commuting hours. Between 2022 and 2024 the trip out of Chía along Avenida Pradilla went from 6 to 10 minutes and the trip in from 8 to 14 minutes (Alcaldía Municipal de Chía, 2024). About 40,000 vehicles use this avenue every day, and in rush hour a trip of 7–10 minutes can take up to 40 (El Cronista, 2026).

The Mobility Secretariat estimates that around half of the vehicles entering Chía are not from residents (Infobae, 2025), and the town already tried measures such as the environmental "pico y placa" for heavy cargo (Alcaldía Municipal de Chía, 2025). Our project looks at the other side: the traffic lights.

We work with a list of four intersections (ID, address, number of phases) and hourly vehicle counts for four junctions. The simulator models one intersection with four approaches and two phases.

Our four phases and CRISP-ML(Q)

CRISP-ML(Q) is a method for machine learning projects with six phases, from understanding the problem to monitoring the model (Studer et al., 2021; ML-Ops.org, n.d.). We organized our work in four phases and each one covers some of those CRISP-ML(Q) phases. The phases are iterative: if we find a problem in one, we can go back to an earlier one. All the detail of each CRISP-ML(Q) phase is in its own page: 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 · 6.

  1. Phase 1 · Group. CRISP-ML(Q): business and data understanding and data engineering. The goal is to reduce the queues of four intersections by adapting the green time. We checked the data: traffic.csv has 48,120 hourly records of four junctions (November 2015 to June 2017), with no empty values, no negative counts and no duplicates, and junction 4 has only 4,344 records against 14,592 of the others. The main risk is that these counts were not measured in Chía, so the prototype works only in simulation. The data loader runs the same steps every time: it fixes the dates, removes invalid or repeated rows, and creates the hour, the day of the week, the weekend flag and a sine-cosine version of the hour, so that 23:00 and 00:00 are close. Then K-Means (k = 3) groups the 168 day-hour slots of the week into off-peak, moderate and peak, for all the intersections together and for each one. The silhouette index is 0.60 in total and between 0.53 and 0.65 per intersection.
  2. Phase 2 · Predict. CRISP-ML(Q): model engineering and model evaluation. Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and an MLP were trained on the same 38,496 rows and tested on 9,624 rows that they never saw (an 80/20 split). The three models pass the R² of 0.8 needed to use them: Random Forest wins with an R² of 0.958 and an error of 2.54 vehicles per hour (Gradient Boosting 0.953 and MLP 0.925). A first version without the intersection and the date as inputs only reached an R² of 0.11; adding them raised it to 0.96, because each intersection has its own level of traffic and traffic almost tripled between 2015 and 2017. Trained only with data before March 2017, the models still reach an R² of about 0.94 on the following months. We used a single split, not cross-validation, so a k-fold validation is still pending. A careful evaluation is needed to trust a model outside the lab (KDnuggets, 2025).
  3. Phase 3 · Decide. CRISP-ML(Q): model engineering and model evaluation. We built a simulator of one intersection (four approaches and two phases; minimum green 8 s, maximum 60 s, yellow 3 s, queue limit 40) and trained a DQN agent in it during 60 episodes of 200 simulated seconds on the four intersections. The reward penalizes long queues and unnecessary changes. To evaluate it we compared it with the 30-second fixed plan on new episodes: over five trainings the average queue went from 10.58 to 7.94 vehicles (25% less on average, between 14% and 33%), with 169.0 vehicles served against 166.2 and no vehicles lost. The agent beat the fixed plan at all four intersections. The results were reviewed with our course professor; a review with the mobility office of Chía is still pending.
  4. Phase 4 · Home. CRISP-ML(Q): deployment and monitoring and maintenance. The models are inside a Flask application with a JSON API, served with gunicorn and published on Render. The dashboard, the simulation and the API need a login (passwords are saved as hashes), while this Home and the CRISP-ML pages are public. The documentation is this page, the CRISP-ML pages and the README. For the monitoring, every training shows the metrics on the dashboard and the live simulation shows the queues of each intersection; a MAE that grows over the reference value of about 2.5 vehicles would tell us that the traffic changed, and the training buttons retrain the models with new data. It is a prototype and does not control real traffic lights.

Eight weeks. Later phases start once the earlier ones give their output (first the groups, then the forecast, then the agent).
