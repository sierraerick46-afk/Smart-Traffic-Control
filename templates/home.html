Smart Traffic Light Control with Artificial Intelligence

The title names the problem (traffic lights that do not adapt to demand), the proposed solution (artificial intelligence) and the scope that was implemented:

  • Problem: fixed-time signals at four intersections of Chía, Cundinamarca.
  • Solution: a model that forecasts the vehicles per hour of each intersection, a rule that turns the forecast into a proposed green time, and a reinforcement learning agent tested in simulation.
  • Implemented scope: a web information system with roles where the forecast becomes a timing plan that a person approves, and a simulator. It is a prototype: it does not control real traffic lights and the counts come from a public dataset assigned to Chía.

Problem and context

In Chía the traffic lights usually repeat the same cycle all day, even when one street is empty and the other is full. Traffic is not constant: it changes with the hour, the day of the week and the type of moment (off-peak, moderate or peak). When the green time does not match the traffic, vehicles wait more than necessary and green time is wasted on empty approaches.

Affected population

Drivers, bus and taxi passengers, cyclists and pedestrians who cross the town, the residents of the neighbourhoods around the main corridors, and the municipal mobility office, which has to plan the signal timings with little information.

Supporting evidence

  • About 40,000 vehicles use Avenida Pradilla every day, and in rush hour a trip of 7–10 minutes can take up to 40 minutes (El Cronista, 2026).
  • Between 2022 and 2024 the trip out of Chía along Avenida Pradilla went from 6 to 10 minutes and the trip in from 8 to 14 minutes (Alcaldía Municipal de Chía, 2024).
  • Around half of the vehicles that enter Chía are not from residents (Infobae, 2025), and the town has already restricted heavy cargo (Alcaldía Municipal de Chía, 2025).
  • In our data the busiest intersection carries on average 45 vehicles per hour and 89 at the 95th percentile, while another carries 7 and 13: one fixed plan cannot fit both.

Consequences

Longer queues and waiting times, more fuel and emissions from idling vehicles, delays for public transport, and signal plans that are changed without data.

Guiding question: how can the traffic lights of four intersections in Chía adapt their green time to the traffic each one receives, without testing unproven ideas on real streets?

Justification and contribution of the intelligent system

  • It uses data instead of a fixed plan. Hourly vehicle counts show patterns by intersection, hour and day that a fixed plan ignores.
  • It forecasts the demand. The selected model forecasts the vehicles per hour with {% if rep %}R² {{ rep.final.test.r2 }} and an average error of {{ rep.final.test.mae }}{% else %}R² 0.904 and an average error of 5.6{% endif %} vehicles per hour on two months it never saw.
  • It supports a decision, it does not replace it. The forecast becomes a proposed green time; an operator adjusts it and an administrator approves or rejects it, and every step is recorded.
  • It learns from reality. The real count is recorded after the hour, the error is compared with the test error and an alert indicates when to retrain.
  • It is safe to try. The reinforcement learning agent is evaluated in a simulator against a fixed 30-second plan{% if ctl %}: it reduced the average queue by {{ ctl.improvement_mean }}% on average over five trainings{% endif %}.

Scope: a prototype that does not control real traffic lights. The counts come from a public hourly dataset assigned to four intersections of Chía as a case study, so the results show how the method works, not the real traffic of Chía.

Develop and publish a web information system that uses machine learning to forecast the hourly traffic of four intersections of Chía and to support the decision of their green time, validating the forecasting model on independent data and the adaptive control in simulation, so that signal timings can be planned with data instead of a fixed plan.

Background and related projects

Fixed-time control. Webster (1958) proposed computing the cycle and the green times from the flows of each approach; the plan is then repeated until someone changes it. Our 30-second fixed plan is the reference against which the agent is compared.

Adaptive systems in operation. SCOOT (Hunt et al., 1982) and SCATS (Sims & Dobinson, 1980) adjust the timings from detectors in the road. They show that adapting the green to demand is useful, but they need detectors and a control centre that Chía does not have; our project uses the hourly counts that are available and a human approval step.

Traffic flow forecasting. Forecasting the flow from its history is a long-standing problem; Lv et al. (2015) showed that learning models improve on classical methods, and Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) explain why the validation must respect time order. We followed both ideas.

Reinforcement learning for signals. Genders and Razavi (2016) and IntelliLight (Wei et al., 2018) trained deep reinforcement learning agents that reduced queues in simulation; the survey of Wei et al. (2019) summarizes this line. Our DQN follows the same approach, also in simulation first.

Theoretical foundations

Unsupervised learning. K-Means puts similar hours in the same group without labels (MacQueen, 1967); we use it to label each hour of the week as off-peak, moderate or peak.

Supervised learning. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting combine many decision trees (Breiman, 2001; Friedman, 2001); the MLP is a neural network trained with backpropagation (Rumelhart et al., 1986). Gradient Boosting was selected.

Reinforcement learning. An agent learns by trial and error to maximize a reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018); a DQN estimates the value of each action with a neural network (Mnih et al., 2015).

Technical concepts

Phase, cycle and green time: the approaches that move together, the sequence of phases, and the seconds a phase keeps the right of way. Queue and flow (vehicles per hour) are the quantities we forecast and reduce.

CRISP-ML(Q) organizes a machine learning project in six phases with quality checks, from business understanding to monitoring (Studer et al., 2021). We applied all six (CRISP-ML pages).

Information system security. Role-based access control assigns permissions to roles instead of to people (Sandhu et al., 1996); passwords are stored as salted scrypt hashes (Percival & Josefsson, 2016) and forms are protected against cross-site request forgery (OWASP Foundation, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

Technological

Python with Flask (Pallets, n.d.) and gunicorn; pandas and NumPy; scikit-learn for K-Means and the regressors (Pedregosa et al., 2011); PyTorch for the MLP and the DQN (Paszke et al., 2019); PostgreSQL in production and SQLite in development; HTML, JavaScript and Bootstrap 5; Git and GitHub with one branch per phase and pull requests; Render for deployment (Render, n.d.).

Contextual

Chía is a municipality of the Sabana Centro region, about 10 km north of Bogotá, and its main corridors fill up at commuting hours (Alcaldía Municipal de Chía, 2024; El Cronista, 2026). We chose four intersections of the town and worked with an hourly count dataset (fedesoriano, 2021) and a traffic-signal inventory (Secretaría Distrital de Movilidad de Bogotá, n.d.) as the model of how a signal list is organized.

How the six CRISP-ML phases were applied

CRISP-ML(Q) is a method for machine learning projects with six phases, from understanding the problem to monitoring the model (Studer et al., 2021; ML-Ops.org, n.d.). We organized our work in four phases and each one covers some of those CRISP-ML(Q) phases. The phases are iterative: if we find a problem in one, we can go back to an earlier one. All the detail of each CRISP-ML(Q) phase is in its own page: 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 · 6.

  1. Phase 1 · Group. CRISP-ML(Q): business and data understanding and data engineering. The goal is to reduce the queues of four intersections by adapting the green time. We checked the data: traffic.csv has 48,120 hourly records of four junctions (November 2015 to June 2017), with no empty values, no negative counts and no duplicates, and junction 4 has only 4,344 records against 14,592 of the others. The main risk is that these counts were not measured in Chía, so the prototype works only in simulation. The data loader runs the same steps every time: it fixes the dates, removes invalid or repeated rows, and creates the hour, the day of the week, the weekend flag and a sine-cosine version of the hour, so that 23:00 and 00:00 are close. Then K-Means (k = 3) groups the 168 day-hour slots of the week into off-peak, moderate and peak, for all the intersections together and for each one. The silhouette index is 0.60 in total and between 0.53 and 0.65 per intersection.
  2. Phase 2 · Predict. CRISP-ML(Q): model engineering and model evaluation. Five models (a seasonal-mean baseline, Ridge regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and an MLP in PyTorch) were trained on time-ordered partitions: training from November 2015 to February 2017 (36,408 rows), validation in March and April 2017 (5,856 rows) and an independent test in May and June 2017 (5,856 rows). Each model was tuned with 5-fold time-series cross-validation, and every preprocessing step (K-Means groups, outlier caps, scaling) was fitted on training data only. Gradient Boosting had the lowest validation error and, refitted on training plus validation, reached an R² of 0.904 and an average error of 5.6 vehicles per hour on the test set, above the 0.8 required by the course professor and 53% less error than the seasonal baseline. A first version without the intersection and the date only reached an R² of 0.11 (details).
  3. Phase 3 · Decide. CRISP-ML(Q): model engineering and model evaluation. We built a simulator of one intersection (four approaches and two phases; minimum green 8 s, maximum 60 s, yellow 3 s, queue limit 40) and trained a DQN agent in it during 60 episodes of 200 simulated seconds on the four intersections. The reward penalizes long queues and unnecessary changes. To evaluate it we compared it with the 30-second fixed plan on new episodes: over five trainings the average queue went from 10.75 to 9.13 vehicles (15% less on average, from −15% to 33%), with 165.4 vehicles served against 163.7 and no vehicles lost. The agent was better in four of the five trainings, so every new agent is checked against the fixed plan before it is used. The results were reviewed with our course professor; a review with the mobility office of Chía is still pending.
  4. Phase 4 · Home and information system. CRISP-ML(Q): deployment and monitoring and maintenance. The saved model (artifacts/model.joblib, featurizer and Gradient Boosting) is loaded by a Flask application served with gunicorn and published on Render with a PostgreSQL database. It is integrated in an information system: users register, log in and have a role (administrator, operator or viewer) enforced by the server; an operator requests a forecast, the server runs the model and stores it with the user and the model version, and the forecast becomes a timing plan that an administrator approves or rejects. The real count is recorded later and compared with the forecast. For the monitoring, a live MAE above 1.5 times the test MAE (about 8.5 vehicles per hour) triggers retraining, and a labelled test with simulated operational data shows the alert working (phase 6). It is a prototype and does not control real traffic lights.

Development and integration of the web information system

The system grew in three steps. First a Flask prototype with a dashboard and a simulator, built in layers (controllers, services, models). Then the complete CRISP-ML process, which produced the saved model (artifacts/model.joblib) with its preprocessing. Finally the information system: a relational database with seven tables (users, intersections, model versions, inferences, timing plans, incidents and logs), registration and login with three roles, and four business processes. The model is integrated on the server: the operator chooses an intersection, date and hour, the server builds the same features used in training, runs the model, stores the inference with the user and the model version, and proposes a green time that follows the plan workflow (draft → pending → approved → applied). Each team member worked on a branch and the changes reached the main branch through pull requests; every merge redeploys the application on Render.
